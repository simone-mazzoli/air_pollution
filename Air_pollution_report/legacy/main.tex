\begin{abstract}

Despite the well-established consequences of air pollution for human health, accurately measuring its spatial distribution remains a challenge. Consequently, precise air monitoring is costly to report at scale. As a result, many regions are undercovered. This gap translates into disparities in the protection measures implemented. In this project, we offer an alternative method to estimate particulate matter (PM) 2.5 using publicly available satellite data. Both a from-scratch CNN and a ResNet50 pre-trained on BigEarthNet are deployed and compared. Additionally, we test the reliability of low-cost PM sensors and compare them to stations of the  European Environmental Agency (EEA) for reference. Finally, the best-performing model is used to create a dense continuous map of PM 2.5 in North-Eastern Germany, which we use to examine social disparities in pollution exposure across the region. 

We find that low-cost sensors are unreliable, even after calibration, and that the from-scratch model consistently achieves better results in the cross-validation arrangement. We argue that the ResNet-50 pretraining is not relevant for this specific task, and that the Sentinel-2 multispectral data modality may not be particularly relevant compared to the other modalities used.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Exposure to fine particulate matter (PM 2.5) is a well-established risk factor for cardiovascular and respiratory diseases, and has been associated with increased mortality rates at concentrations around the current regulatory thresholds in many countries (WHO air quality guidelines 2021). The estimation of PM in an area constitutes a fundamental step to introduce public health policies and targeted interventions.

High-quality PM measurements are, however, costly to produce. Many regions in Europe are undercovered. This creates discrepancies and mistreatments in public intervention between extensively covered areas and sparsely covered ones. Such undercovered regions correlate with lower 
income areas and lower population density, further marginalizing the already vulnerable communities.

%verify correlation above mentioned in socioeconomic analysis

Recent studies have examined the validity of low-cost sensors as a scalable yet imprecise alternative to high quality reference station measurements (Benabbas et al, 2019). The overall reliability is disputed. After analysing their validity, we argue that such sensors do not capture PM well enough to be used as labels for our models. We therefore rely on measurements from EEA reference stations to train and fine-tune models capable of predicting PM 2.5 from a wide range of satellite data modalities.

Our approach addresses the limited spatial coverage of conventional monitoring networks by using satellite data as model inputs. Unlike reference stations, publicly available satellite observations provide coverage across regions where precise ground-based PM measurements are unavailable, allowing the trained model to generate more spatially continuous estimates of PM 2.5. We apply this approach to North-Eastern Germany, which serves as the target region of this study.

\subsection{Pipeline Overview}

Figure 1 shows the pipeline overview. After downloading all necessary data, the first step is to evaluate the validity of low-cost sensors and determine whether they are suitable for use as labels in our models. in the final pipeline, we demonstrate their unreliability and therefore resort to EEA-wide reference station measurements.

After that, we co-locate all satellite patches with their respective PM measurements at the given locations. Since we perform point estimation, PM2.5 is predicted at the center of each patch, where the corresponding monitoring station is located.


The paired labels and patches are then used to train both models from scratch and fine-tuned ones. We show that the models trained from scratch systematically outperform the ResNet-50 models pretrained on BigEarthNet. Possible explanations include the relative dissimilarity between our task and the land-cover classification task used to pre-train the ResNet-50, as well as the characteristics of the satellite patches used for training.

The best-performing model is then evaluated over North-Eastern Germany, and a continuous PM prediction map is generated. The characteristics and limitations of this map are discussed in the respective section.

\section{Data Collection and Processing}

In this section, we describe the data sources used in this project and the processing steps applied to produce valid, model-ready training data.

We use three main categories of data: particulate matter (PM) measurements, satellite patches (including pollution covariates), and socioeconomic data. Figure 1 provides an overview of the different data types used. Unless otherwise specified, the data refer to the year 2024.

%figure with overview of data types 

\subsection{PM Data}

We gather PM measurements that serve as labels for our models to train. Low-cost (LC) sensor measurements are gathered from Sensor.Community, while reference stations across Europe (EEA stations) are fetched using the EEA API. Sensor.Community is a non-profit organisation that collects data from private sensors. They supply information for how private actors can install a home sensor and collect the data from sensors connected to their network. They provide access to approximately ten thousand sensors globally. The EEA collects data from local and regional authorities and creates uniform datasets with environmental data across Europe. The Sensor.Community sensors are abundant and cover many areas not extensively covered by the EEA stations, while the latter provide high quality measurements to test the low cost sensors' validity.


%preprocessing decisions 
The EEA stations are filtered to keep only the stations that have valid values for at least 90\% of the days of 2024. Invalid values include missing or negative values, values that have been determined invalid by the stations themselves and those stations that do not have all the necessary satellite and covariate data. %how many stations are filtered out by this process.  
The PM values are then averaged over the full year to create annual averages for each station. They are log-transformed and standardized based on the training set mean and standard deviation. Outliers above a value of 120 ug/m3 PM10 and 25 ug/m3 PM2.5 are removed.

\subsection{Pollution Covariates}

In addition to S2 imagery, we incorporate four auxiliary covariates through the same GEE pipeline. Sentinel-5P (S5P) atmospheric column data provide tropospheric NO2 and CO measurements (both 5 × 5 pixels, 7 km/px), as well as aerosol index data over a wider spatial extent (31 × 31 pixels, 7 km/px). The latter motivates our choice of a 100 km buffer distance, which is intended to avoid spatial leakage from this data. S5P patches are generated as cloud-filtered annual mean composites. In addition, we obtain elevation data from the Copernicus GLO-30 digital elevation model (DEM) (15 × 15 pixels, 1 km/px).

\subsection{Socio-Economic Data}

To examine whether modeled air-pollution exposure is spatially associated with
socioeconomic and demographic characteristics, we construct a dataset at the
German Kreis level. The dataset covers all 400 Kreise and combines information
from several official sources. Population, age structure, median age, and
population density refer to 2024 and are obtained from Eurostat. Kreis-level
unemployment rates for 2024 are obtained from the German Federal Employment
Agency. Disposable household income per capita is taken from the Regional
Accounts of the German States (VGRdL) and refers to 2023. Finally, indicators
of immigration history, nationality, and educational attainment are derived
from the 2022 German Census. Administrative Kreis identifiers and boundaries
are based on official BKG geodata.

Because the most recent available observation year differs across data
sources, we retain the original reference year of each variable. The socioeconomic variables
therefore describe broadly contemporaneous, but not perfectly synchronous
conditions between 2022 and 2024.

For the subsequent analysis, we focus on disposable income, unemployment,
educational attainment, immigration history, population density, and age
structure. Immigration history is treated as a demographic characteristic
rather than as an indicator of socioeconomic disadvantage. Population density
is included because urbanicity may be associated with both socioeconomic
characteristics and pollution exposure.

Figure~\ref{fig:socioeconomic_distributions} shows the distribution of the
selected indicators across German Kreise. Population density is shown on a
logarithmic axis because its distribution is strongly right-skewed, with a
small number of highly urbanized Kreise having much larger densities than the
majority of districts.

The CNN created from scratch is a multi-model that assembles several models for each of the data inputs. The model analyzing the high resolution satellite patches has four blocks. In each of the four blocks, the channel size increases across each block, in the following sequence: 48, 96, 192, 384. Each block contains 3 sequences of: a 3 by 3 convolution, a batch normalization, and a ReLU function. At the end of the block, the output is reduced using a maxpool to make sure the channel number remains stable within each of the model blocks. After all four blocks all channels are pooled to one average number, which is the model output. The model that analyses the low resolution satellite patches, has only 2 blocks, that are apply the sequence of what happens in high resolution block but only once. So, first a 3 by 3 convolution, a batch normalisation and a ReLU. The second block will jump directly to 128 channels. In between both block there is a maxpool as well. The model for the sp5 images has a smaller input dimension of 2$\times$5$\times$5 (two stacked channels — NO2 and CO — at 5×5 pixels, 7 km/pixel) and uses 32 channels throughout. It has the same sequence of steps in its 2 blocks as the low resolution cnn, but without the maxpool in between. Each branch is normalized independently before fusion, preventing differences in output scale from dominating the combined representation. In addition to the convolutional branches, a small four-value scalar vector (\texttt{xs\_mean}) is fed directly into the regression head: the spatial mean of the NO2 patch, the spatial mean of the CO patch, the center-pixel value of the wide aerosol context patch, and station elevation in km. This gives the head direct access to raw scalar context alongside the learned convolutional features.

\subsection{Fine-tuned Model}

The fine-tuned variant replaces the scratch high-resolution encoder with a ResNet-50 pre-trained on BigEarthNet, a large Sentinel-based remote-sensing dataset. Specifically, it was originally pre-trained to classify land-cover usage. It is important to highlight that only high resolution S2P patches pass through this model, every other data modality remains the same scratch CNN modules described in subsection 4.1. In other words, this variant tests whether features learned for land-cover classification transfer usefully to pixel-level PM 2.5 regression, or whether the two tasks are different enough that training an encoder from scratch on the pollution data directly is preferable.

\subsection{Training}

Both models are trained under identical leave-one-fold-out cross-validation: eight geographically defined development folds. Each take a turn as the validation fold, with the model trained on the remaining seven. Training stations within 100 km of any validation station are removed from that fold's training set to prevent spatial leakage between neighbouring, highly correlated locations. The northern/eastern German TEST region is never used at this stage; it is sealed until the final evaluation in Section~\ref{sec:results} (Section~\ref{sec:results}.2). Both models are trained with early stopping (patience 25 epochs, maximum 500), selecting the epoch with the lowest validation RMSE per fold.

Reliability is highly uneven across the TEST region (Table~\ref{tab:test_by_state}). Berlin is the only state with positive R\textsuperscript{2} (0.235) and the lowest bias, plausibly because its higher station density and urban pollution profile are better represented in the development folds. Bremen's extreme R\textsuperscript{2} ($-26.0$) is largely a small-sample artefact ($n=4$) rather than evidence of poor model behaviour there, and should not be weighted equally with the larger states. Sachsen-Anhalt shows both the largest bias (+2.94) and worst error (MAE 3.11), making it the state where predictions should be treated most cautiously. In general, predictions are most trustworthy in and around dense, well-covered urban areas and least trustworthy in the sparsest rural stretches of the TEST region -- precisely the areas a deployed model would be most valuable for, and precisely where we can vouch for it least.

This directly bears on the ethics of acting on predicted rather than measured pollution: the model is not uniformly reliable across the region it is meant to serve, and any policy use of the resulting map should weight predictions by local reliability (e.g. distance to the nearest training station, or state-level performance as in Table~\ref{tab:test_by_state}) rather than treating the continuous 
map in Section~\ref{sec:results}.3 as equally trustworthy everywhere.

\subsection{Implementation of Dense Grid Map}

We apply our best performing model to create a continuous map of North-Eastern Germany by firstly predicting point estimates across the region on a regular grid spaced at 10 km intervals. To obtain a smooth, continuous surface from these point estimates we construct a Delaunay triangulation over the grid points. We therefore connect each point to its adjacent neighbours to form a triangle mesh, and we interpolate within each triangle based on the values of the three grid points constituting the triangle vertices. The result is a continuous visual surface of PM 2.5 predictions across north-eastern Germany. We argue it is reasonable to interpolate values across a 10km span using triangle meshes, as pollution at that granularity tends to vary smoothly, except in the immediate vicinity of highly localized emission sources.

This is a promising sign that the model does not simply associate high pollution with population density alone, but incorporates other relevant factors such as wider spatial context and the aerosol index covariate.


Overall, there is tendency to overestimate PM values in this region. Figure~\ref{fig:test_residual_map} plots the values. While significant and worthy of attention, we argue that this systematic bias does not change the relative order of intervention priority in the region, and the predictions therefore remain useful to policy makers -- keeping in mind the state-level reliability differences reported in Table~\ref{tab:test_by_state}.
